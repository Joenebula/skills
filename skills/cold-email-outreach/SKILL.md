# cold-email-outreach — specific, short, and few

The one core truth: **a cold email works only if the reader can tell, in the first line, that a person looked at *their* business.** A template with the name swapped in reads as spam to the reader and to the spam filter. Everything below serves that line.

Sending is outward-facing and cannot be undone. Drafting is the job; the user sends ([[ask-dont-guess]]). Consent and opt-out law sit in [[privacy-and-compliance]]. System email (receipts, resets) is a different lane: [[email-and-notifications]].

Where a project has its own approved wording (subject, offer sentence, sign-off), **that wording wins.** This skill gives the shape; the project gives the words. Find the project's rules first (its CLAUDE.md, audit README, or memory) and follow them.

## Law 1 — Check you may send at all

- **UK (PECR):** a limited company or LLP may get a cold email if it has a clear opt-out. A sole trader or a partnership needs consent first. If you cannot tell which it is, say so and do not draft a send.
- Every email carries a working opt-out line, and a "stop" reply is honoured at once and recorded.
- Never guess an address. Use one found on the business's own site. Prefer a named person, then info@ or hello@, then sales@.

## Law 2 — The first email has five parts, in this order

1. **Greeting** — the person's name if known, otherwise "Hi there,".
2. **One specific thing**, in one or two sentences, said as **the cost to the owner in their own words**. It must be something only a person who looked could know.
   - Good: "Your phone number is hard to find, so a customer who wants to book has to hunt for it."
   - Bad: "Your site has low contrast." "Your site looks dated." Any UX, SEO or WCAG term.
3. **What you have for them**, in one sentence (the review, the idea, the sample).
4. **An interest question, not a meeting request.** "Would it help if I talked you through it? It takes 10 minutes." Not "When are you free?" and not a calendar link.
5. **Sign-off.**

Keep it to about five lines before the sign-off. Leave out of email 1: prices, past-client results, a list of fixes, images, and the word "free". One idea per email.

## Law 3 — The subject line is short and about them

- 3–7 words, naming their business or site. No clickbait, no "Re:" on a first email, no all caps, no "free".
- If the project has an approved subject, use it unchanged.

## Law 4 — Follow-ups: at most two, and each one gives something

- Reply on the **same thread**, about 4 days apart.
- Each follow-up is 1–2 lines and adds **one new useful thing**: follow-up 1 a second specific point, follow-up 2 a short proof (a past result), **only if it is true and the user approved it**.
- Never write "just checking in", "following up on my last email", "bumping this", or "sorry to bother you".
- Stop after email 1 plus two follow-ups. If three emails did not get a reply, the targeting or the message needs work, not a fourth email. More emails cause spam reports, and spam reports hurt every later send.
- No follow-up if they replied, opted out, or bounced.

## Law 5 — Protect deliverability

- Every email is written for its one reader. Never send the same body to many leads with only the name changed.
- Plain text. No images in the body, no tracking pixels, at most one or two links.
- An attachment on a first cold email raises spam risk. If the project attaches one by choice, keep it small and say so when reviewing results; test link-only if replies stay low.
- Send in small numbers from a real, warmed mailbox. Never buy a list.

## Law 6 — Review before it goes

Before handing a draft to the user, check:

- [ ] The sender may legally send to this business (Law 1).
- [ ] Line 2 names a real thing on their site that you saw yourself (not from memory, not guessed).
- [ ] No jargon; a busy owner gets it in one read.
- [ ] One question, and it asks about interest.
- [ ] The project's approved wording is used word for word.
- [ ] Opt-out line present.
- [ ] Any new client-facing wording is shown to the user before it is saved or sent.
